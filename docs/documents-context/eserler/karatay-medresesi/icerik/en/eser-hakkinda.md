# Karatay Madrasa · About (English)

Translation of `../eser-hakkinda.md`, paragraph by paragraph. Sources and trust labels come from the Turkish record.

## Tarihçe | History

The madrasa is at Ankara Caddesi No. 1, Selçuklu, on the northern foot of Alâeddin Hill. In the Seljuk age this area formed an important central square, together with the Sultan Gate of the inner citadel and other nearby educational buildings.

According to the inscription above its portal, the Karatay Madrasa was built in AH 649 / AD 1251. Its founder, Emir Celaleddin Karatay, had the madrasa bearing his name built during his time as atabeg, directly across from the Kemaliye Madrasa of his elder brother, Emir Sipehsâlar Kemaleddin Rumtaş.

Although the madrasa has no artist's inscription, Muhammed bin Havlan el-Dımışkî, also architect of Alâeddin Mosque, is thought to have worked on it. The two-coloured marble, "Zengid knots" and decorative composition of the door jambs seen at the north portal of Alâeddin Mosque also appear on the Karatay portal. The side niches at the courtyard entrance of Aksaray Sultan Han, another work of this Damascene architect, share a similar design with the Karatay portal.

## Vakfiye | Endowment deed

The building's endowment deed of AH 651 / AD 1253–54 says that Celaleddin Karatay built a madrasa "tall in structure, wide in area, fine in ground, refreshing to the eye and joyful to people, as if in the pavilions of paradise". It required the teacher and his assistant to be of the Hanafi school and masters of Sharia, hadith, jurisprudence and disputation. There was no school requirement for students, and the rule was five days of lessons a week with two days off, Tuesday and Friday.

## Mimari ve süsleme | Architecture and decoration

The madrasa's mixed stone and brick masonry walls use mortar as a binder and timber as bond beams. Brickwork can be seen in the original dome and transition elements.

The portal's decoration of white and grey marble, made by carving, inlay and interlacing, shows Syrian influence. The striking interior decoration uses tile as mosaic, openwork and plate facing.

The closed-courtyard madrasa is covered by a hemispherical dome 12 m across, resting on the courtyard walls. Reached at the corners through fan-shaped pendentives, the dome has a skylight at its centre.

In this single-iwan madrasa the portal is set back to the southern corner of the east façade, outside the axis of symmetry between iwan and portal. The building's east façade also breaks inward with a chamfer at its northern edge.

## Bugün | Today

By the early 20th century all parts of the madrasa except the courtyard, the iwan and the tomb at the southwest corner lay in ruins. The four facing student rooms along the north and south sides, which survive through repairs and changes, are now barrel-vaulted exhibition halls with their partition walls removed.

The room south of the madrasa's iwan is the tomb of Celaleddin Karatay, closed by a herringbone brick dome reached through Turkish triangles.

The building has been used as the Museum of Tile Works since 1955. The museum displays tile, plaster and glass works from the Kılıçarslan II Pavilion, Kubadabad Palace and other Seljuk buildings in Konya, such as the Sırçalı Madrasa and Abdülaziz Mosque, along with examples of Kütahya and Çanakkale ceramics.
