# Habib-i Neccar Mosque · About (English)

Translation of `../eser-hakkinda.md`, paragraph by paragraph. Sources and trust labels come from the Turkish record.

## Tarihçe | History

In its history, architecture and decoration, Habib-i Neccar Mosque is among the most distinguished historic buildings in Antakya. With these qualities, it seems to convey with its whole being that it was built in the name of a religious figure who belongs to history.

According to one account, Antakya is where Islam began to spread in Anatolia, and the first mosque built in Anatolia is also in Antakya. Habib-i Neccar Mosque was built in AD 636 by Abu Ubayda bin Jarrah, one of Caliph Umar's commanders. As the first person to believe in the Apostles of Jesus, Habib-i Neccar is a monument of faith and a martyr praised in the Qur'an, in the Surah Yâsin. His grave is inside the mosque.

In the polytheistic period, Jesus sent envoys to Antakya so that the Roman people might believe in God. The graves of the envoys John, Paul and Simon Peter are also believed to be in the mosque. The mosque is a contemporary of the Great Mosque not only in size and fame but also in date. As at the Great Mosque, its oldest surviving inscription sheds some light on the past with the title "al-Malik al-Zahir" of the famous Mamluk sultan Baybars, who freed the city from Mongol rule in 1268. Inscriptions and archival documents show that the Habib-i Neccar complex was repaired many times, the last with the help of the people. Another contemporary of the mosque is Şeyh Ali Mosque.

Information from the Antakya yearbooks traces the mosque's history back to Abu Ubayda bin Jarrah, the famous commander of the first Muslim conquests. Although nothing has been found of the work he is assumed to have built, even this tradition is telling: it shows that the first Muslims recognised Habib-i Neccar's religious standing and remembered him with reverence.

Since Habib-i Neccar, who gives the mosque its name, is thought to be "one of the envoys calling the people to faith" in the story told in verses 13–29 of the Surah Yâsin, and the events are thought to have taken place in Antakya, it seems reasonable that Muslims built a mosque here.

That a person connected with the disciples of Jesus is remembered well in the Qur'an, and that a sanctuary built in his name by Christians was revived by Muslims, shows the deep roots and meaning of how people of any faith live in peace in Antakya. The mosque has come down to us through repairs and alterations under the Mamluks in 1474 and the Ottomans in 1721, 1858 and 1863. Today the complex consists of the mosque, madrasa and fountain, with the tombs of Habib-i Neccar and Saint Paul.

In the trapezoidal courtyard, the mosque is placed to the south, madrasa rooms to the east, northeast and northwest, and the fountain to the north. The tombs are on the east side of the mosque.

## Cami | The mosque

The crown portal on the east of the courtyard has a monumental appearance. A muqarnas cornice beginning at the springing of the arch surrounds the hood on three sides. A little above it is the inscription.

The six-bay late-comers' portico, kept somewhat lower than the high-domed prayer hall on the south of the courtyard, gives the building a dynamic rhythm. As such, the work became the representative of classical Ottoman taste in Antakya. The twenty-four-sided rounded shaft on a square base at the east end of the arcade, with its umbrella-shaped cap, is important for carrying on the traces of regional architecture. Habib-i Neccar Mosque resembles Şeyh Ali Mosque not only in the shape and arrangement of its courtyard but also in the plan and architecture of its prayer hall and in the place and shape of its minaret.

The door of the prayer hall, built of white cut stone, with black and white stones, is set back within a tall pointed-arched hood. The mosque has a rectangular plan extended east to west. The area before the mihrab is covered by a dominant dome on a high polygonal drum. The areas to its east and west are each covered by a cross vault. The dome, side walls, late-comers' portico and arcade are entirely covered with metal sheet.

The mihrab, projecting slightly from the qibla wall, is a niche with a pointed-arched hood. A window is set inside the niche. The door of the marble minbar, just to the right of the mihrab, is covered by a dome carried on arches resting on squat columns with muqarnas capitals at the balustrades. The minbar has all the elements of a classical one, with its pavilion and the part below it.

A gallery is set against the north inner wall of the prayer hall, reached by spiral stairs inside the wall through a door opening onto the window just to the right of the entrance.

## Medrese | Madrasa

The madrasa consists of seven cells on the north of the courtyard of Habib-i Neccar Mosque, four on the west and one directly to the east, below the imam's room. The cells bordering the courtyard are covered by a hipped roof. Each room on the north and west sides opens onto the courtyard with a door and a window.

That the single cell in the east wing has two windows onto the street and is larger than the others may indicate that it was the teacher's room. The madrasa has no decoration; one of its many inscriptions shows that it was built in 1863 by a man named Halil Ağa. The others are repair inscriptions.

## Şadırvan | Fountain

The fountain on the north of the courtyard consists of a twelve-sided water tank, short columns rising from its corners and a broad pyramidal cap covered with metal sheet resting on them. Above the tank, the body stands out for the window-like arched opening of its niche, palmette-like tassels, and many horizontal Baroque cornices in the upper part, continuing up to the eaves.

Another element of the fountain worth mentioning is its four-line construction inscription, dated AH 1270 (AD 1858).

## Habib-i Neccar Türbesi | Tomb of Habib-i Neccar

The tomb on the east side of the mosque, cross-vaulted and trapezoidal in plan, has a ground floor opening through two windows each to the east and south, and consists of two floors reached by stairs.

These vaulted spaces each hold two cenotaphs, set somewhat to the side rather than exactly in the middle. That Christians also hold Habib-i Neccar sacred is why the mosque's history is carried back to the 1st century. Christian visits to the tomb suggest that it was first built even earlier than this date.

Of the sarcophagi in today's tomb, one lies on a south–north axis and the other on an east–west axis.

The oldest document showing a repair specific to the tomb dates from 1857.

## Aziz Pavlos'un Türbesi | Tomb of Saint Paul

Just south of the complex's east entrance, the tomb on a rectangular area contains two cenotaphs side by side, believed to belong to the Prophet John and to Paul. The covering of the rectangular area with a mirror vault is another notable feature of the building.

There is no clear information about when the tomb was first built, but the position of the cenotaphs suggests they were placed according to Islamic beliefs.
